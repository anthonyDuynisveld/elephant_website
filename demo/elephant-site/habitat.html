Animal Habitat

African elephants are found across sub-Saharan Africa and inhabit a remarkably diverse range of environments. They are highly adaptable animals that can thrive in many different ecosystems, provided they have access to sufficient food, water, and space to roam.

Where on Earth Are They Found?

African elephants are found in 37 countries across sub-Saharan Africa. The largest populations exist in Botswana, Tanzania, Zimbabwe, Kenya, South Africa, and Zambia. Historically, they ranged across nearly the entire continent, but their range has been significantly reduced due to habitat loss and human expansion.

Where Do They Make Their Home?

Unlike many animals, elephants do not have a fixed home or den. They are nomadic, traveling across vast areas following seasonal food and water sources. Their home ranges can span from 15 to over 3,000 square kilometers depending on resource availability.

Natural Surroundings

African elephants thrive in a variety of habitats:

  • Savannas Open grasslands with scattered trees are the classic elephant habitat. The African bush elephant is most commonly found here, grazing on grasses and browsing on trees.
  • Tropical Forests The African forest elephant inhabits the dense tropical rainforests of central and western Africa. These forests provide shade, moisture, and a rich supply of fruit.
  • Woodlands Miombo and mopane woodlands provide elephants with access to both browse and grass, making them ideal mixed-feeding habitats.
  • Marshes & Wetlands Elephants frequent swamps and river deltas such as the Okavango Delta in Botswana, where water and lush vegetation are plentiful year-round.
  • Semi-Arid Regions Desert-adapted elephants in Namibia's Damaraland and Mali's Sahel region survive in extremely dry conditions, traveling great distances between water sources.
  • Montane Habitats Elephants can be found at elevations up to 5,000 meters on Mount Kilimanjaro and in the mountain forests of Kenya and Uganda.

What Do They Need to Thrive?

Elephants require large areas of land with reliable water sources, diverse vegetation, and corridors connecting different habitat patches. They are "ecosystem engineers" that shape their environment by knocking down trees, digging for water, and creating pathways that other animals use. Their survival is closely tied to the health of the habitats they depend on, making conservation of wild spaces essential to their future.
